An ETF rotation strategy that survived an honest re-test

The original backtest earned 27% a year. Once trading costs were charged, that edge was gone. A longer signal window, chosen only on 2014 to 2021 data, brought it back and kept working from 2022 to 2026.

What I did: rebuilt a trend-following ETF rotation strategy on public data, added trading costs, and chose its one parameter on 2014 to 2021 data only.

    How the signal works

    RSRS stands for resistance support relative strength. For each ETF, take the last N days and regress the daily high on the daily low. A steep slope means highs are rising faster than lows, which reads as an uptrend. The score is that slope multiplied by the regression's R squared, so a trend only counts when the fit is clean.

    Every evening the strategy ranks six ETFs by score and holds only the top one the next day. The six cover large caps, mid caps, dividend stocks, ChiNext growth stocks, the Nasdaq 100 and gold.

    The slope equals cov(high, low) divided by var(low), and R squared equals the squared correlation, so the whole score is computed with rolling pandas operations instead of fitting thousands of regressions.

    What made the backtest more honest

    • Bad prices removed. Yahoo data had moves larger than the exchange's daily price limit. Short spikes that reversed within days were dropped, and unadjusted early history was cut off. Four fixes in total.
    • Only shared trading days. If one ETF misses a day, shifting the signal by one row would put it on the wrong date, so only days when all six traded are kept.
    • Costs on every switch. 0.03% commission plus 0.05% slippage per side, charged twice on a switch since one ETF is sold and another bought. Trades happen at the open after each signal.

    Choosing the window without looking at the test period

    I ran the strategy for 13 window lengths and picked the one with the best Sharpe ratio from 2014 to 2021. That was N = 30. Only after the choice did I look at 2022 to 2026.

      Limitations

      • Gold and the Nasdaq 100 were held most of the time, and both had strong runs in this period. Part of the return is exposure to those two assets, not the signal alone.
      • The six ETFs were picked with hindsight. A fairer test would pick the universe by a rule fixed in advance.
      • There is one train and test split. Walk-forward testing, re-choosing N every year, would be a stronger check.
      • Holding one ETF at a time gives drawdowns above 20%. Blending the top two or three would likely be smoother.
